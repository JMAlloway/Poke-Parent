Avoiding Fakes & Safe Buying

TL;DR

  • Fake Pokémon cards exist and are more common than you might think, especially online.
  • You can spot most fakes by checking the card feel, color saturation, font details, and the light test.
  • Buy from authorized retailers, local game stores, and reputable online sellers.
  • Red flags: prices that seem too good to be true, stock photos on listings, and "mystery packs" from unknown sellers.

Why Fakes Are Worth Knowing About

Counterfeit Pokémon cards are manufactured in large quantities and sold cheaply, often through online marketplaces. They range from obvious (blurry printing, wrong card sizes) to surprisingly convincing at first glance. A child probably will not notice or care about the difference, but if your family is spending real money, you deserve to get real cards.

How to Spot Fakes

Feel and Texture

Real Pokémon cards have a specific feel. They are made of layered cardstock with a particular stiffness and a smooth but not slippery finish. Fakes often feel thinner, flimsier, or oddly waxy. If you have a card you know is real, hold it next to the one in question. The difference in thickness and flexibility is usually noticeable.

Color Saturation

Fake cards tend to have colors that are slightly off — too bright, too dark, or washed out compared to genuine cards. The blue on the card back is one of the easiest things to compare. Real Pokémon card backs have a consistent, specific shade of blue. If the back looks noticeably different from cards you already own, that is a warning sign.

Font and Text Details

Look closely at the text on the card. Fakes sometimes have spelling errors, slightly wrong fonts, or text that is bolder or thinner than it should be. The Pokémon logo, set symbol, and card number in the bottom corner are all areas where fakes frequently have small but visible mistakes.

The Light Test

Hold the card up to a bright light (a phone flashlight works well). A genuine Pokémon card has an opaque black layer sandwiched between the front and back layers of cardstock. Very little light should pass through. A fake card, which is usually just printed on regular paper or thin card, will let light through much more easily. This is one of the simplest and most reliable tests.

Where to Buy Safely

  • Authorized retailers. Big-box stores, major online retailers that source directly from distributors, and Pokémon Center (the official Pokémon online store) are all safe. If the product is sealed and purchased from a legitimate retailer, it is real.
  • Local game stores (LGS). Small shops that specialize in trading card games are generally trustworthy. They depend on their reputation and often have knowledgeable staff who verify cards they buy and sell. Supporting them also supports your local community.
  • Reputable online card shops. Established online sellers with long track records, clear return policies, and verified reviews are generally safe for both sealed products and individual cards. Look for shops that have been around for years, not ones that appeared last month.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Too-good-to-be-true prices. If a booster box retails for $140 and someone is selling it for $50, something is wrong. Counterfeiters sell at low prices because their production cost is extremely low. A significant discount from market price on a current, in-demand product is one of the strongest warning signs.
  • Stock photos on marketplace listings. If a seller on eBay, Facebook Marketplace, or similar platforms is using official product images instead of photos of the actual item they are selling, be cautious. Legitimate sellers typically photograph their own inventory.
  • "Mystery packs" from unknown sellers. Repackaged "mystery" or "value" packs from sellers you have never heard of are a common way to move fake or bulk low-value cards at a markup. Stick with factory-sealed products from known sources.
  • No return policy. Reputable sellers stand behind their products. If a seller does not offer returns or makes the process intentionally difficult, consider shopping elsewhere.

What to Do If You Get a Fake

If you suspect cards you received are counterfeit, contact the seller and request a refund. Most marketplace platforms (eBay, Amazon, etc.) have buyer protection policies that cover counterfeit goods. Save the cards as evidence until the dispute is resolved. If you bought in person and the seller will not help, leave an honest review so other parents can make informed decisions.

And if your child has already grown attached to a card that turns out to be fake, that is okay. Let them keep it if they want to. The enjoyment was real, even if the card was not.
